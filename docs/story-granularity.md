# What one story is

The rule for grouping articles into stories, used for matching prompts and for the hand-checked answer key in `fixtures/match-eval/`. When the prompts and this guide disagree, change the prompt.

A **story** is one development: something that happened, or one wave of the same kind of response to it. A reader should be able to say "this story is about X" in one short sentence, and every article in it should be about that X.

## Same story

- **The same event, reported by different publishers**, in any language, with any headline or angle.
- **Updates to that event:** a rising death toll, new details, a correction of a figure, a fuller account the next morning.
- **An official statement about the official body's own action**, and the coverage of that action. For example, a ministry announcing a ban, and news reports of the ban.
- **Live blogs and roundups** whose main subject is the event.

## New story

- **A new event of the same kind**, even a day later and in the same place. A second strike on the same airport the next day is a new story.
- **A distinct move in response:** a counter-strike, sanctions, a lawsuit, a resignation, a policy change, a vote. It's linked to the event, but separate from it.
- **Consequences that are events themselves:** an airport closing for days, flights cancelled, a match postponed.
- **Analysis and explainers** ("Why are Houthi missiles getting through?") that argue or explain rather than report the event. They stay separate unless they mostly recap it.

## One story for a wave of similar reactions

**Statements of the same kind by several actors**, about the same event, form **one** story, separate from the event itself. Examples:

- condemnations or expressions of support by governments and organisations
- condolences
- travel warnings issued by several countries

So "UK condemns", "China condemns" and "Nigeria condemns" the Riyadh airport attack are **one** story: "Countries condemn the Riyadh airport attack". The attack is a different story.

A reaction that **does** something stays separate, even if it also condemns: a country announcing sanctions, sending troops or recalling an ambassador.

## Worked examples (October 2026)

**Riyadh airport attacks:**
- **Story 1:** the first missile attack, which killed 12. Updates to the toll join it.
- **Story 2:** the second strike the next day, including "Saudi Arabia reports injuries in new attack at Riyadh airport, day after strike".
- **Story 3:** countries condemn the attacks: UK, China, Nigeria, Pakistan, Turkey, Egypt…
- **Story 4:** the airport closure and flight cancellations.
- **Story 5:** the US considers joining Saudi strikes on the Houthis.
- **Separate stories, not merged into the attacks:** analysis pieces ("Saudi Arabia has few options…", "Why are Houthi missiles breaking through…").

**Ukraine "energy ceasefire":**
- **Story 1:** Trump announces the energy ceasefire.
- **Story 2:** Russia hits Ukrainian power plants despite the announced ceasefire. This is a new event, not an update.
- **Story 3:** the EU says it will welcome a truce "when it holds", along with similar statements by other actors. That's a wave of reactions.

**Hurricane Simon:** landfall and damage reports form one story. Mexico's president urging coastal residents to stay indoors is the government's own response to the same event, so it joins too. A later "death toll rises" report joins as well. Aid pledges by several countries form one separate reactions story.

## When unsure

Prefer **separate** stories that the relationship graph links, over one story that mixes two developments. A wrong merge corrupts a summary; a missed merge only splits coverage, and the narrative still connects it.
